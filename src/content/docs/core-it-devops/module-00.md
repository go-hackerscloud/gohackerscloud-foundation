## What you will be able to demonstrate

- Explain the key concepts in your own words, not only repeat definitions.
- Draw or describe where this capability is used in real IT systems.
- Collect basic, authorized evidence from a guided exercise.
- Explain at least one common failure and the safest next troubleshooting step.

## Core concepts and why they matter

### Learning goals vs. evidence

A goal such as 'understand networking' is difficult to measure. A verifiable outcome—draw a browser-to-server request and explain the role of DNS, TCP and TLS—lets you see what you know and what needs another attempt.

### Learning in deliberate stages

Begin with physical and virtual systems, then networking, Linux and Windows, then programming and Git. Each builds context for the next. A shortcut to an advanced lab without that context often produces copied commands without understanding.

### Self-paced Foundation and hosted labs

Foundation pages are free reading, illustrations, self-checks and suggested independent exercises. Academy membership is a separate product; only particular lessons with authorized launches provide hosted practice under their access terms.

### Your personal learning notebook

Record definitions in your own words, commands you ran (without secrets), observations, diagrams, failed attempts and the next question. Revisit a topic after one day and one week. This is evidence of learning, not an automatically graded Academy dashboard.

## Enterprise case study (hypothetical)

A college graduate hears that cybersecurity requires Linux, Python, AWS and penetration testing. Instead of buying several courses immediately, the learner designs a three-week trial: explain one network flow, inspect a Linux service on a personal VM and write a tiny Python summary. Each week ends with a one-page evidence note.

### How the engineer should think

1. Confirm the scope and what you are authorized to access.
2. Separate facts, hypotheses and assumptions before recommending changes.
3. Choose one observation that can distinguish likely causes.
4. Record output, timestamps and the limitations of each test.
5. Escalate with evidence when a change requires production permission.

## Guided self-study exercise

Complete these tasks on your own system, in a classroom with permission, or using paper diagrams. Hosted Academy labs are not required for this free module.

1. Write a 90-day goal and a smaller seven-day milestone.
2. Choose two Core IT modules and one concept from each to explain in plain English.
3. Write a three-line notebook entry: expected outcome, observed result and what remains unclear.
4. Choose a peer or friend to whom you can explain the concept in two minutes.

### Expected observations and how to verify

A learning plan that names your starting knowledge, target role, two weekly sessions, and an observable outcome. If you are new, diagrams and written explanations are sufficient; no software installation is mandatory.

### Troubleshooting if results differ

Do not treat an error as failure of the learning goal. Check whether the command exists on your operating system, whether it needs permissions and whether the environment (desktop, WSL, container or VM) behaves differently. Record the observation without making unnecessary system changes.

## Common mistakes and professional judgment

- Starting with advanced tools before understanding network and OS concepts
- Mistaking reading time for demonstrated ability
- Assuming free Foundation pages include paid memberships or hosted labs

## Portfolio-ready evidence

**Deliverable:** A one-page study plan, mini skills matrix and first learning reflection.

Store the exercise as a self-study artifact. Include purpose, scope, assumptions, tools, observations, conclusions and limitations. Do not call a simulated lab a production incident.
